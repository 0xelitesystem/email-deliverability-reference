# Why email lands in spam

Sending an email does not mean it arrives in the inbox. Between your send and the recipient reading it, the mailbox provider, the company running the recipient's email, decides whether the message belongs in the inbox, the spam folder, or nowhere at all. Deliverability is the practice of earning that decision in your favor, and it starts with understanding what the provider is actually trying to do.

## The provider works for the recipient, not the sender

The key mental shift is that the mailbox provider's job is to protect its users from unwanted mail, not to deliver yours. Its incentive is to put mail the recipient wants in the inbox and keep everything else out, because that is what keeps users happy with the email service. Every deliverability rule follows from this. You do not get the inbox by demanding it or by sending more; you get it by being the kind of sender the provider believes its users want to hear from. Fighting the provider's judgment loses; aligning with it wins.

## What the provider is judging

Providers weigh three broad things. First, are you who you claim to be? Spoofing and forgery are rampant, so providers check whether the message is authenticated as genuinely from your domain, covered on the next page. Second, do you have a good reputation as a sender? Providers track how mail from your domain and sending source has been received over time, and treat a trusted sender differently from an unknown or troubled one. Third, do recipients actually want this mail? Engagement signals, opens, replies, and the absence of complaints and deletions, tell the provider whether its users value your messages.

## Spam folder versus blocked entirely

There are two distinct bad outcomes. Landing in the spam folder means the message arrived but was filtered, so a recipient who looks can still find it. Being blocked or silently dropped means it never reached the mailbox at all, with no folder to rescue it from. Blocking is the more serious failure and usually reflects a reputation or authentication problem severe enough that the provider refused the mail outright. Knowing which is happening matters, because spam-foldering is often a content and engagement issue while outright blocking is usually an authentication or reputation issue.

## It is mostly earned, not configured

A common hope is that deliverability is a setting you switch on. The authentication on the next page is configuration, and it is necessary, but the rest is earned behavior over time: sending to people who want your mail, keeping complaints low, and building a track record the provider trusts. There is no toggle that forces the inbox. The senders who reliably reach inboxes are the ones whose recipients consistently want and engage with their mail, which the provider can see. The rest of this reference is about becoming and staying that kind of sender.
