# Content and formatting that helps

After authentication, reputation, and a healthy list, the message itself plays a smaller but real role in deliverability. Some content and formatting choices make filters wary, and some make recipients more likely to engage, which feeds back into reputation. None of this rescues a message from a bad reputation, but it avoids self-inflicted problems on top of an otherwise good standing.

## Write like a person, not a billboard

Filters and recipients both respond better to mail that reads like genuine communication than like a hard advertisement. The hallmarks of spammy content, which both filters and people react against, include shouting in all capitals, excessive exclamation marks, classic spam phrases promising money or miracle results, and a wall of large flashing imagery. Plain, clear, conversational writing that delivers on what the recipient signed up for tends to both pass filters and earn engagement. The goal is mail a real person wrote to a real audience, because that is what the whole system is tuned to reward.

## Balance text and images

A message that is almost entirely one large image, with little real text, is a long-standing spam pattern, because spammers used images to hide their text from filters. Mail that is all image and no text, or that hides its message inside a graphic, looks suspicious. A healthy balance of actual text with supporting images, where the message is readable even if images do not load, is both safer with filters and better for recipients, many of whom have images off by default or read on devices where a giant image is awkward. Write the message as text first, and let images support it rather than carry it.

## Mind the links and the from address

Links matter to filters. Mail pointing to a domain with a poor reputation, using link shorteners that obscure the destination, or stuffed with many links can look suspicious. Linking to your own established domain and keeping the link count reasonable is safer. The from address matters too: a recognizable, consistent sender name and address that recipients associate with the mail they asked for builds familiarity, while a constantly changing or obscure from address undermines it. Recipients decide in a moment whether mail is wanted partly from who it appears to be from, so a stable, recognizable identity helps both engagement and trust.

## The subject line and the first impression

The subject line is where the recipient decides whether to open, and opening is the engagement signal that protects deliverability. A subject that honestly reflects the content and gives the recipient a reason to open earns opens; a misleading or clickbait subject earns opens once and complaints thereafter, as recipients feel tricked. Deceptive subjects are a fast route to the spam button. An honest, relevant subject that matches what the recipient signed up for is both more sustainable and, over time, better for open rates, because recipients learn that your mail is worth opening rather than learning to distrust it.

## Test before you send to everyone

Before a large send, it is worth checking how the message renders and whether it lands in the inbox across the major providers, because a message that looks fine to you can break or trip filters elsewhere. Sending a test to addresses you hold at the main providers, and checking where it lands and how it looks, catches problems before they reach your whole list. This is cheap insurance against discovering, after the fact, that a campaign went to spam or rendered badly for a large share of recipients. Combined with everything earlier, authentication, reputation, a healthy engaged list, honest and well-formed content, this is what reliably earns the inbox.
