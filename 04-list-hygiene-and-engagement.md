# List hygiene and engagement

The single biggest lever on deliverability is who you send to. Providers infer how much recipients want your mail from how those recipients behave, so a smaller list of people who open and engage reaches the inbox far more reliably than a larger list full of people who ignore or report you. Deliverability rewards quality of audience over quantity, which is the opposite of the instinct to mail as many people as possible.

## Engagement is the strongest signal

When recipients open your mail, read it, reply, or click, providers read that as evidence the mail is wanted, and they keep delivering it to the inbox. When recipients ignore it, delete it without reading, or mark it as spam, providers read that as evidence it is unwanted, and they start filtering it, not just for those recipients but increasingly for everyone. This means disengaged recipients are not harmless dead weight; they actively drag down your deliverability to the people who do want your mail. Engagement is the currency, and a list that does not engage spends down your reputation with every send.

## Permission is the foundation

Engagement starts with permission. People who genuinely asked to hear from you engage; people who did not, do not, and some report you as spam, which is the most damaging signal of all. This is why purchased lists, scraped addresses, and adding people who did not opt in are so destructive: they fill your list with recipients who never wanted your mail, generating complaints and non-engagement that wreck deliverability for everyone. A clear, genuine opt-in, where people knowingly choose to receive your mail, is the basis of a healthy list. The permission-email reference in this portfolio covers the consent side in more depth.

## Remove the disengaged

Because disengaged recipients hurt deliverability, periodically removing or suppressing people who have not engaged in a long time improves your inbox placement, even though it shrinks the list. This feels counterintuitive, paying to grow a list and then deliberately removing part of it, but the math favors it: a list of engaged recipients reaches the inbox, while the same list padded with dead addresses reaches spam. Before removing long-inactive recipients entirely, a re-engagement attempt asking whether they still want your mail can recover some, and those who do not respond are exactly the ones dragging you down. Pruning the disengaged is maintenance, not loss.

## Handle bounces and complaints promptly

Sending to addresses that no longer exist produces bounces, and a high bounce rate signals a poorly maintained list, which hurts reputation. Removing addresses that hard-bounce immediately keeps your list clean and your bounce rate low. Likewise, when a recipient marks your mail as spam, the right response is to stop mailing them at once, which most email services handle automatically through complaint feedback. Continuing to mail someone who complained compounds the damage. Treating bounces and complaints as immediate signals to remove an address, rather than ignoring them, is basic list hygiene that protects everything else.

## Make leaving easy

It seems backward, but making it easy to unsubscribe protects deliverability. A recipient who no longer wants your mail has two options: unsubscribe, which is harmless, or mark you as spam, which is damaging. A clear, working, one-click unsubscribe steers people toward the harmless option and away from the complaint. Hiding or complicating the unsubscribe pushes frustrated recipients to the spam button instead, which hurts you far more than losing a subscriber would. An easy exit is not a concession; it is a way to keep your complaint rate low and your reputation intact, and it keeps your list composed of people who actually want to be on it.
